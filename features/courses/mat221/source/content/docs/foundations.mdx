## Antiderivative families

An antiderivative of $f$ is a function $F$ whose derivative is $f$:

$$
F'(x)=f(x).
$$

Because the derivative of every constant is zero, one function has a whole family of antiderivatives:

$$
\boxed{\int f(x)\,dx=F(x)+C}.
$$

For example, $x^2$, $x^2+4$, and $x^2-100$ all differentiate to $2x$. Therefore

$$
\int2x\,dx=x^2+C.
$$

## Two questions integration answers

Integration answers two related questions:

- Which function has the given derivative?
- How much quantity has accumulated over an interval?

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus connects these questions: antiderivatives calculate accumulated change.

## Properties used throughout the course

Linearity allows sums and constant multiples to be integrated term by term:

$$
\int[af(x)+bg(x)]\,dx=a\int f(x)\,dx+b\int g(x)\,dx.
$$

Reversing bounds changes the sign, and an interval of zero width has zero integral:

$$
\int_b^a f(x)\,dx=-\int_a^b f(x)\,dx,
\qquad
\int_a^a f(x)\,dx=0.
$$

## Basic antiderivative rules

For $n\ne-1$,

$$
\boxed{\int x^n\,dx=\frac{x^{n+1}}{n+1}+C}.
$$

The exceptional exponent $n=-1$ gives a logarithm:

$$
\boxed{\int\frac{dx}{x}=\ln|x|+C}.
$$

Also remember

$$
\int e^x\,dx=e^x+C,
\quad
\int\cos x\,dx=\sin x+C,
\quad
\int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C.
$$

### Worked example: combine rules without skipping algebra

Evaluate

$$
\int\left(4x^6-\frac3{x^4}+e^{-3x}\right)dx.
$$

First rewrite $3/x^4$ as $3x^{-4}$. By linearity,

$$
\int4x^6dx-\int3x^{-4}dx+\int e^{-3x}dx.
$$

Now apply each rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int4x^6dx&=\frac47x^7,\\
-\int3x^{-4}dx&=-3\left(\frac{x^{-3}}{-3}\right)=x^{-3},\\
\int e^{-3x}dx&=-\frac13e^{-3x}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\frac47x^7+\frac1{x^3}-\frac13e^{-3x}+C}.
$$

The factor $-1/3$ in the exponential term compensates for the inner derivative $-3$.

## FTC Part 1: differentiating accumulated area

If $f$ is continuous, then

$$
\boxed{\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt=f(x)}.
$$

The upper limit moves. Increasing it by a small amount adds a thin strip whose height is approximately $f(x)$, so the rate of accumulation is $f(x)$.

If the upper limit is another function, use the chain rule:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)}f(t)\,dt=f(g(x))g'(x).
$$

For example,

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\int_2^{x^2}(3t^2+1)dt
=(3x^4+1)(2x).
$$

## FTC Part 2: evaluating a definite integral

If $F'=f$ on $[a,b]$, then

$$
\boxed{\int_a^bf(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)}.
$$

For $\int_1^32x\,dx$, use $F(x)=x^2$:

$$
\int_1^32x\,dx=[x^2]_1^3=3^2-1^2=\boxed{8}.
$$

Do not add $C$ to a final definite value: the constants cancel in $F(b)-F(a)$.

## Common errors

- Forgetting $+C$ for an indefinite integral.
- Using the power rule when $n=-1$.
- Writing $F(b)+F(a)$ instead of $F(b)-F(a)$.
- Applying the ordinary FTC across a discontinuity or blow-up.
- Forgetting the chain-rule factor when a limit is $g(x)$.
