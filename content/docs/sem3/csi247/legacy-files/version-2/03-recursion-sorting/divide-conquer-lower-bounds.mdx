## 3.2 Divide & Conquer Recurrences & Lower Bounds

To analyze Divide and Conquer algorithms, we translate their recursive structure into recurrence relations. Furthermore, we must understand the absolute mathematical limits of comparison-based sorting.

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Recurrence Analysis: Merge Sort"
  description="Merge Sort divides the array in half, sorts recursively, and merges in linear time."
>

**Recurrence Relation:**
$$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n)$$
- **Divide:** $O(1)$
- **Conquer:** $2T(n/2)$
- **Combine:** $\Theta(n)$ to merge two sorted halves.

**Solution:** Using the Master Theorem ($a=2, b=2, f(n)=n$), $c_{crit} = \log_2 2 = 1$. Since $f(n) = \Theta(n^{c_{crit}})$, Case 2 applies: $T(n) = \Theta(n \log n)$.

</HighlightCard>

<HighlightCard
  variant="neutral"
  title="Recurrence Analysis: Quick Sort"
  description="Quick Sort partitions the array around a pivot and sorts the partitions recursively."
>

**Average Case Recurrence:**
Assuming the pivot divides the array roughly in half:
$$T(n) = 2T(n/2) + \Theta(n) \implies T(n) = \Theta(n \log n)$$

**Worst Case Recurrence:**
If the pivot is the minimum or maximum element, the partition is highly unbalanced (sizes $0$ and $n-1$):
$$T(n) = T(n-1) + \Theta(n)$$
This expands to the arithmetic series $\sum_{i=1}^n i = \Theta(n^2)$.

</HighlightCard>

<StepByStep>
  <StepByStepTitle>Mandatory Proof: The Decision Tree Lower Bound</StepByStepTitle>

  <Step number={1}>
    <StepTitle>The Theorem</StepTitle>
    Any comparison-based sorting algorithm requires $\Omega(n \log n)$ comparisons in the worst case.
  </Step>

  <Step number={2}>
    <StepTitle>The Model</StepTitle>
    A comparison sort can be modeled as a binary decision tree. Each internal node represents a comparison $A[i] \le A[j]$, and each leaf represents a possible permutation of the input.
  </Step>

  <Step number={3}>
    <StepTitle>Counting Leaves</StepTitle>
    To correctly sort all possible inputs, the tree must have at least one leaf for every permutation of the $n$ distinct elements. Thus, the number of leaves $L \ge n!$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={4}>
    <StepTitle>Relating Height to Leaves</StepTitle>
    A binary tree of height $h$ has at most $2^h$ leaves. Therefore, $2^h \ge L \ge n!$.
  </Step>

  <Step number={5}>
    <StepTitle>Solving for Height (Bounding Argument)</StepTitle>
    Taking the base-2 logarithm: $h \ge \log_2(n!)$.
    We expand the logarithm of the factorial:
    $$\log_2(n!) = \log_2(1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 \cdots n) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log_2 i$$
    To find a lower bound, we drop the first half of the terms (since all terms are positive):
    $$\sum_{i=1}^n \log_2 i \ge \sum_{i=\lceil n/2 \rceil}^n \log_2 i$$
    In this remaining sum, there are at least $n/2$ terms, and each term is at least $\log_2(n/2)$. Therefore:
    $$\sum_{i=\lceil n/2 \rceil}^n \log_2 i \ge \frac{n}{2} \log_2\left(\frac{n}{2}\right) = \frac{n}{2} (\log_2 n - 1) = \Omega(n \log n)$$
    Thus, $h \ge \Omega(n \log n)$, proving the worst-case number of comparisons is $\Omega(n \log n)$. $\blacksquare$
  </Step>
</StepByStep>
