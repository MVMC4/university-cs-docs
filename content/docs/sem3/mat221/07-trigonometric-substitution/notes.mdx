<Concept title="Trigonometric substitution" what="A change of variable that converts a quadratic radical into a trigonometric square." why="Pythagorean identities collapse the radical and expose standard trigonometric integrals." when="Use it for powers of a²−x², a²+x², or x²−a² when an ordinary u-substitution does not remove the radical." />

<Theorem title="Quadratic-radical matching" statement={"\\(a^2-x^2:\ x=a\\sin\\theta;\\quad a^2+x^2:\ x=a\\tan\\theta;\\quad x^2-a^2:\ x=a\\sec\\theta\\)"} conditions="Choose a domain where the resulting radical has the stated sign, transform dx, and return to x after integration." meaning="The sign pattern—not preference—selects the identity and substitution." consequences="A reference triangle converts remaining trigonometric functions back to algebraic expressions in x." />

The required properties are

$$
1-\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta,
\quad
1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta,
\quad
\sec^2\theta-1=\tan^2\theta.
$$

| Radical | Substitute | Radical becomes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ | $x=a\sin\theta$ | $a\cos\theta$ |
| $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ | $x=a\tan\theta$ | $a\sec\theta$ |
| $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$ | $x=a\sec\theta$ | $a\tan\theta$ |

## Complete transformation checklist

After choosing the substitution, rewrite:

1. every occurrence of $x$;
2. the radical;
3. $dx$;
4. the bounds, if the integral is definite.

## Worked example

Evaluate

$$
\int\frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{x^2+4}}.
$$

The pattern is $x^2+a^2$ with $a=2$, so let

$$
x=2\tan\theta,\qquad dx=2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta,
$$

and

$$
\sqrt{x^2+4}=\sqrt{4\tan^2\theta+4}=2\sec\theta.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{x^2+4}}
&=\int\frac{2\sec^2\theta}{(4\tan^2\theta)(2\sec\theta)}\,d\theta\\
&=\frac14\int\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin^2\theta}\,d\theta\\
&=-\frac1{4\sin\theta}+C.
\end{aligned}
$$

From $\tan\theta=x/2$, draw a triangle with opposite side $x$, adjacent side $2$, and hypotenuse $\sqrt{x^2+4}$. Therefore

$$
\sin\theta=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+4}},
$$

and the final answer is

$$
\boxed{-\frac{\sqrt{x^2+4}}{4x}+C}.
$$

## Second worked example: the $a^2-x^2$ pattern

Evaluate

$$
\int\sqrt{9-x^2}\,dx.
$$

The radical has the form $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ with $a=3$, so state the matching identity

$$
1-\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta
$$

and choose

$$
x=3\sin\theta,\qquad dx=3\cos\theta\,d\theta.
$$

On $-\pi/2\le\theta\le\pi/2$, $\cos\theta\ge0$, so

$$
\sqrt{9-x^2}=\sqrt{9-9\sin^2\theta}=3\cos\theta.
$$

Therefore

$$
\int\sqrt{9-x^2}\,dx=9\int\cos^2\theta\,d\theta.
$$

Now state the half-angle identity before using it:

$$
\cos^2\theta=\frac{1+\cos2\theta}{2}.
$$

Thus

$$
9\int\cos^2\theta\,d\theta
=\frac92\theta+\frac94\sin2\theta+C.
$$

Since $\theta=\sin^{-1}(x/3)$ and $\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta=2x\sqrt{9-x^2}/9$,

$$
\boxed{\int\sqrt{9-x^2}\,dx
=\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{9-x^2}+\frac92\sin^{-1}\left(\frac{x}{3}\right)+C}.
$$

<Callout label="Common error" tone="trap">Do not stop in $\theta$. The original problem was in $x$, so use a reference triangle or an inverse-trig relation to return to $x$.</Callout>

## Common mistakes

- Matching the wrong sign pattern to sine, tangent, or secant.
- Transforming the radical but forgetting to transform $dx$.
- Dropping the absolute-value or domain condition when simplifying a square root.
- Leaving the answer in $\theta$ when the original variable was $x$.
