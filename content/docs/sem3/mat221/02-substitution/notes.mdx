# Integration by Substitution

<ConceptBlock title="What substitution does">

Integration by substitution is a change of variable that replaces a composite expression and its differential with $u$ and $du$. It reverses the chain rule and turns a composite integral into a basic antiderivative.

</ConceptBlock>

<TheoremBlock title="Substitution rule">

If $g$ is differentiable and the complete integral can be rewritten in one variable, then

$$
\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du,qquad u=g(x).
$$

For a definite integral,

$$
\int_a^b f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx
=
\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f(u)\,du.
$$

</TheoremBlock>

## Why the rule works

The chain rule says

$$
\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x))=F'(g(x))g'(x).
$$

Reading this backwards gives

$$
\int F'(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=F(g(x))+C.
$$

The substitution $u=g(x)$ and differential $du=g'(x)dx$ make that reverse-chain-rule structure visible.

<RecognitionStrategy>

Look for:

- a function inside another function, such as $\cos(x^2)$;
- a denominator together with its derivative;
- an exponent together with its derivative;
- a trigonometric function together with its derivative;
- a repeated expression that becomes simpler when renamed.

After choosing $u$, the entire integral must use only $u$ and $du$.

</RecognitionStrategy>

## Constant factors

If $du=2x\,dx$ but the integral contains only $x\,dx$, then

$$
x\,dx=\frac12du.
$$

The factor one half is required. It cannot be ignored merely because the substitution pattern is otherwise correct.

## Eight worked examples

<WorkedExampleSet examples={substitutionExamples} />

<CommonMistake title="Variable mixing">

An expression such as $\int x\sqrt{u}\,du$ is not a completed substitution. Rewrite $x$ in terms of $u$, or reconsider the choice of substitution.

</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake title="Definite-bound mismatch">

Never evaluate a $u$-antiderivative at old $x$-bounds. Either change the bounds and remain in $u$, or back-substitute and use the original bounds—not both.

</CommonMistake>
