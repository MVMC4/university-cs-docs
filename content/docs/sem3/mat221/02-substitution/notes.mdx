## Why the rule works

The chain rule says

$$
\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x))=F'(g(x))g'(x).
$$

Reading this backwards gives the substitution rule:

$$
\boxed{\int F'(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=F(g(x))+C}.
$$

If $u=g(x)$, then $du=g'(x)\,dx$, so the same rule is written

$$
\boxed{\int f(g(x))g'(x)\,dx=\int f(u)\,du}.
$$

## Recognition strategy

Look for these signals:

- a function inside another function, such as $\cos(x^2)$ or $\sqrt{4-x^2}$;
- a denominator together with its derivative, such as $x/(1+x^2)$;
- an exponent together with its derivative, such as $xe^{x^2}$;
- a trigonometric function together with its derivative, such as $\sin^3x\cos x$;
- a repeated expression that would make the integral simpler if renamed.

<Callout label="Method test">After choosing $u$, you must be able to rewrite the entire integral using only $u$ and $du$. If an $x$ remains, either rewrite it in terms of $u$ or reconsider the substitution.</Callout>

## Properties used by substitution

Substitution relies on:

1. the chain rule;
2. the differential relation $du=g'(x)\,dx$;
3. linearity, which allows constant factors to move outside an integral;
4. the power, logarithmic, exponential, and trigonometric antiderivative rules after the transformation.

For example, if $du=2x\,dx$ but the integral contains only $x\,dx$, then

$$
x\,dx=\frac12du.
$$

The factor $1/2$ is required by linearity; it cannot be ignored.

## The complete method

1. Choose the inner expression $u=g(x)$.
2. Differentiate it: $du=g'(x)\,dx$.
3. Solve for the exact factor that appears in the integral.
4. Rewrite every part of the integral in $u$.
5. Integrate with respect to $u$.
6. For an indefinite integral, substitute $u=g(x)$ back.
7. For a definite integral, change the bounds and remain in $u$.
8. Check by differentiating or estimating the definite answer.

## Worked example 1: exact derivative match

Evaluate

$$
\int2x\cos(x^2)\,dx.
$$

The inner function is $x^2$, and its derivative $2x$ is present. Let

$$
u=x^2.
$$

Differentiate:

$$
du=2x\,dx.
$$

Now replace both the inner expression and its differential:

$$
\int2x\cos(x^2)\,dx=\int\cos u\,du.
$$

Use the basic rule $\int\cos u\,du=\sin u+C$:

$$
\int\cos u\,du=\sin u+C.
$$

Return to $x$:

$$
\boxed{\sin(x^2)+C}.
$$

Check using the chain rule:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\sin(x^2)=\cos(x^2)(2x),
$$

which is the original integrand.

## Worked example 2: a missing constant factor

Evaluate

$$
\int xe^{x^2}\,dx.
$$

Let $u=x^2$, so

$$
du=2x\,dx\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad x\,dx=\frac12du.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int xe^{x^2}\,dx
&=\frac12\int e^u\,du\\
&=\frac12e^u+C\\
&=\boxed{\frac12e^{x^2}+C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<Callout label="Why the half matters" tone="check">Differentiating $e^{x^2}$ produces $2xe^{x^2}$. Multiplying by $1/2$ cancels that extra $2$.</Callout>

## Worked example 3: rewriting a leftover $x$

Evaluate

$$
\int x\sqrt{x+1}\,dx.
$$

Choose $u=x+1$. Then

$$
du=dx,\qquad x=u-1.
$$

The second relation is necessary because the original integral still contains $x$. Rewrite everything:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int x\sqrt{x+1}\,dx
&=\int(u-1)u^{1/2}\,du\\
&=\int(u^{3/2}-u^{1/2})\,du\\
&=\frac{2}{5}u^{5/2}-\frac{2}{3}u^{3/2}+C.
\end{aligned}
$$

Return to $x$:

$$
\boxed{\frac25(x+1)^{5/2}-\frac23(x+1)^{3/2}+C}.
$$

## Worked example 4: logarithmic structure

Evaluate

$$
\int\frac{dx}{x\ln x}.
$$

The expression $\ln x$ is inside the denominator, and its derivative $1/x$ is present. Let

$$
u=\ln x,\qquad du=\frac{dx}{x}.
$$

Then

$$
\int\frac{dx}{x\ln x}=\int\frac{du}{u}=\ln|u|+C.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\ln|\ln x|+C}.
$$

The absolute value belongs to the outer logarithm because the standard property is $\int du/u=\ln|u|+C$.

## Worked example 5: definite substitution

Evaluate

$$
\int_0^1\frac{x}{1+x^2}\,dx.
$$

Let

$$
u=1+x^2,\qquad du=2x\,dx,\qquad x\,dx=\frac12du.
$$

Change the bounds immediately:

$$
x=0\Rightarrow u=1,\qquad x=1\Rightarrow u=2.
$$

Now the entire calculation stays in $u$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^1\frac{x}{1+x^2}\,dx
&=\frac12\int_1^2\frac{du}{u}\\
&=\frac12[\ln|u|]_1^2\\
&=\frac12(\ln2-\ln1)\\
&=\boxed{\frac12\ln2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Because the integrand is positive on $[0,1]$, a positive answer is reasonable.

## Common errors

- Choosing $u$ but never computing $du$.
- Dropping a constant factor such as $1/2$.
- Mixing $x$ and $u$ in the transformed integral.
- Using old $x$-bounds on a $u$-antiderivative.
- Back-substituting in a definite integral after already changing the bounds.
- Forgetting $+C$ in an indefinite integral.
