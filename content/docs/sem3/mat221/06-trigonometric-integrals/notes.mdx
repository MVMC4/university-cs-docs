## Identities used in this chapter

Pythagorean identities:

$$
\sin^2x+\cos^2x=1,
\qquad
1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x.
$$

Double-angle identities:

$$
\cos2x=1-2\sin^2x=2\cos^2x-1.
$$

Solving them for the squares gives the half-angle, or power-reduction, identities:

$$
\boxed{\sin^2x=\frac{1-\cos2x}{2}},
\qquad
\boxed{\cos^2x=\frac{1+\cos2x}{2}}.
$$

They are used when both sine and cosine powers are even because no single sine or cosine factor is available as a substitution differential.

## Powers of sine and cosine

For $\int\sin^m x\cos^n x\,dx$:

- If $m$ is odd, save one $\sin x$, convert the remaining sine powers using $\sin^2x=1-\cos^2x$, then use $u=\cos x$.
- If $n$ is odd, save one $\cos x$, convert the remaining cosine powers using $\cos^2x=1-\sin^2x$, then use $u=\sin x$.
- If both powers are even, use the half-angle identities.

## Worked example: odd sine power

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int\sin^3x\cos^2x\,dx
&=\int(1-\cos^2x)\cos^2x\sin x\,dx.
\end{aligned}
$$

Let $u=\cos x$, so $du=-\sin x\,dx$:

$$
-\int(u^2-u^4)\,du
=\boxed{\frac15\cos^5x-\frac13\cos^3x+C}.
$$

## Worked example: both powers even

For $\int\sin^2x\,dx$, use

$$
\sin^2x=\frac{1-\cos2x}{2}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\boxed{\int\sin^2x\,dx=\frac{x}{2}-\frac{\sin2x}{4}+C}.
$$

<Callout label="Why not use 1-cos²x?">When the sine power is even, replacing $\sin^2x$ by $1-\cos^2x$ usually leaves no $\sin x\,dx$ for $u=\cos x$. The half-angle identity changes the frequency and makes the integral elementary.</Callout>

## Powers of secant and tangent

For $\int\sec^m x\tan^n x\,dx$:

- If the secant power is positive and even, save $\sec^2x\,dx$, convert the rest with $\sec^2x=1+\tan^2x$, and use $u=\tan x$.
- If the tangent power is positive and odd, save $\sec x\tan x\,dx$, convert the remaining tangents with $\tan^2x=\sec^2x-1$, and use $u=\sec x$.
- For a pure even power of tangent, repeatedly use $\tan^2x=\sec^2x-1$.
- For an odd power of secant alone, use integration by parts.

For example,

$$
\boxed{\int\tan^2x\,dx=\int(\sec^2x-1)\,dx=\tan x-x+C}.
$$

## Products with different angles

When the integrand contains products such as $\sin5x\cos4x$, use product-to-sum identities:

$$
\sin A\cos B=\frac12[\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B)].
$$

Thus

$$
\int\sin5x\cos4x\,dx
=-\frac12\cos x-\frac1{18}\cos9x+C.
$$

## Common mistakes

- Applying the ordinary power rule directly to a trigonometric power.
- Converting the saved odd factor instead of the even remainder.
- Forgetting the chain factor after a multiple-angle identity.
- Using product-to-sum when a shorter parity substitution is available.
