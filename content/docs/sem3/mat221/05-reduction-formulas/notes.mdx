<Concept title="Reduction formula" what="A recurrence that expresses an integral I_n using easier members such as I_(n-1) or I_(n-2)." why="One derivation solves an entire family and replaces repeated integration with a controlled step-down process." when="Use it when an integer power varies and integration by parts or an identity reliably lowers that power." />

<Theorem title="A recurrence is not a final value" statement={"\\(I_n=F(n,x)+c_nI_{n-k}\\)"} conditions="The step k must be positive, the valid n-range must be stated, and the appropriate base case must be known." meaning="Derivation and evaluation are separate: first prove the recurrence, then iterate it to a base case." />

## Complete method

1. Define the family $I_n$.
2. Apply integration by parts so the power decreases.
3. Rewrite the remaining integral as $I_{n-1}$ or $I_{n-2}$.
4. Isolate $I_n$ and state the valid values of $n$.
5. Evaluate the necessary base case.
6. Step down until the base case is reached.

<Callout label="Key idea">A formula is only a reduction formula if the new index is smaller. A relation involving $I_{n+1}$ has not simplified the problem.</Callout>

## Example 1: $I_n=\int x^ne^x\,dx$

Choose $u=x^n$ and $dv=e^x\,dx$. Then

$$
du=nx^{n-1}\,dx,\qquad v=e^x.
$$

Integration by parts gives

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_n
&=x^ne^x-n\int x^{n-1}e^x\,dx\\
&=\boxed{x^ne^x-nI_{n-1}},\qquad n\ge1.
\end{aligned}
$$

The reduction is by one, so the base case is

$$
I_0=\int e^x\,dx=e^x+C.
$$

To evaluate $I_3$, keep the chain visible:

$$
\begin{aligned}
I_3&=x^3e^x-3I_2\\
&=x^3e^x-3(x^2e^x-2I_1)\\
&=x^3e^x-3x^2e^x+6(xe^x-I_0)\\
&=\boxed{e^x(x^3-3x^2+6x-6)+C}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Differentiate the boxed answer. All lower-power terms cancel, leaving $x^3e^x$.

## Example 2: a recurrence that lowers by two

Let

$$
I_n=\int\cos^n x\,dx.
$$

Write $\cos^n x=\cos^{n-1}x\cos x$ and choose

$$
u=\cos^{n-1}x,\qquad dv=\cos x\,dx.
$$

Then

$$
du=-(n-1)\cos^{n-2}x\sin x\,dx,\qquad v=\sin x.
$$

After parts and $\sin^2x=1-\cos^2x$,

$$
I_n=\sin x\cos^{n-1}x+(n-1)I_{n-2}-(n-1)I_n.
$$

Collect the $I_n$ terms:

$$
\boxed{I_n=\frac1n\sin x\cos^{n-1}x+\frac{n-1}{n}I_{n-2}},\qquad n\ge2.
$$

For $I_5$, the odd chain stops at $I_1=\sin x$:

$$
\boxed{I_5=\frac15\sin x\cos^4x+\frac4{15}\sin x\cos^2x+\frac8{15}\sin x+C}.
$$

## Example 3: definite sine powers

Define

$$
J_n=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n x\,dx.
$$

Integration by parts gives

$$
\boxed{J_n=\frac{n-1}{n}J_{n-2}},\qquad n\ge2.
$$

There are two base cases because the recurrence steps down by two:

$$
J_0=\frac{\pi}{2},\qquad J_1=1.
$$

Even powers reach $J_0$, while odd powers reach $J_1$. Thus

$$
J_6=\frac56J_4=\frac56\cdot\frac34J_2
=\frac56\cdot\frac34\cdot\frac12J_0
=\boxed{\frac{5\pi}{32}}.
$$

Now evaluate the lecture application:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^6x(1+\cos^2x)\,dx
&=\int_0^{\pi/2}(2\sin^6x-\sin^8x)\,dx\\
&=2J_6-J_8\\
&=2\left(\frac{5\pi}{32}\right)-\frac78\left(\frac{5\pi}{32}\right)\\
&=\boxed{\frac{45\pi}{256}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

<Callout label="Base-case rule" tone="check">If the recurrence lowers by one, expect one base case. If it lowers by two, parity matters and you may need both $I_0$ and $I_1$.</Callout>

## Definite boundary terms

For $I_n=\int_1^e x(\ln x)^n\,dx$, take $u=(\ln x)^n$ and $dv=x\,dx$. The boundary term is

$$
\left[\frac{x^2}{2}(\ln x)^n\right]_1^e=\frac{e^2}{2}\qquad(n\ge1),
$$

so

$$
\boxed{2I_n=e^2-nI_{n-1}}.
$$

Do not reuse that boundary value at $n=0$: the lower endpoint is then nonzero. Evaluate $I_0=(e^2-1)/2$ directly.

## Common mistakes

- Reporting a recurrence without its valid range or base case.
- Reducing in the wrong direction so the index increases.
- Mixing the even and odd base-case chains.
- Reusing a boundary term at an index where its endpoint value changes.
