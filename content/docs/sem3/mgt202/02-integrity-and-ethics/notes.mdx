## Why integrity and ethics matter

Success is usually measured in profit, sales, growth, customers, market share and innovation. How a business achieves it matters just as much, because every decision affects other people: customers, employees, owners, suppliers, government, communities and the natural environment. Business decisions are therefore not purely financial. They also raise questions of right and wrong, fairness, honesty, responsibility and trust.

Suppose a large customer asks you to put false information on an invoice. There are two issues: the financial opportunity (a lot of income) and the ethical issue (the request is dishonest). The question is not only "Will this make money?" but also "Is this decision honest, fair and responsible?"

## What integrity is

<KeyTerm title="Integrity">
A general sense of honesty and reliability, expressed through a strong commitment to doing what is right regardless of the circumstances. A person's values, beliefs, words, decisions and actions are reasonably consistent with one another.
</KeyTerm>

The central idea is doing the right thing even when it is difficult, inconvenient or costly. The deck puts the test as two questions: not only "Will I be caught?" but also "Is this the right thing to do?"

| Characteristic | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Honesty | Being truthful in dealings with others | Accurate information to customers, correct financial records, admitting mistakes |
| Reliability | Doing what you say you will do | Making every reasonable effort to deliver by the promised Friday |
| Consistency | Applying the same standards when circumstances change | Not valuing honesty only when it is convenient |
| Responsibility | Accepting responsibility for decisions and actions | Not automatically blaming employees, customers, suppliers, competitors or government |
| Commitment to doing what is right | Acting rightly when it involves sacrifice | Refusing a profitable but dishonest request |

### Integrity is tested under pressure

Behaving ethically is easy when everyone is watching, there is little to gain, there is no pressure and the right choice is obvious. The real test comes when nobody is supervising you, you could benefit personally, the business is under financial pressure, others seem to be acting dishonestly, or you believe you will not be caught. The class uses three small tests of this:

- The P200 change: a cashier gives you P200 too much. Keep it, return it, or wait and see? Does your answer change if the cashier may have to cover the shortage?
- The honesty experiment: you flip a coin four times alone, each favourable result earns a reward, and nobody can verify what you report. Would your answer change if the reward grew?
- A runaway train heads for five people and you can divert it to one. The point is to say what you would do and, more importantly, why.

## Ethics and integrity are not the same

<KeyTerm title="Ethics">
Questions and standards about right and wrong, fair and unfair behaviour, and responsible and irresponsible action.
</KeyTerm>

<Callout type="key">
Ethics tells us what we should do. Integrity is shown when we consistently act on those principles. An owner who knows that falsifying records is wrong, but falsifies them under financial pressure, understands the ethics and fails the test of integrity.
</Callout>

The notes group workplace misconduct into three areas: **customers and competitors**, **the treatment of employees**, and **employees' obligations to their employers**.

<Callout type="note" title="Textbook">
The textbook shows a chart of the most frequently observed forms of workplace misconduct: abusive behaviour, lying to employees, violations of internet use policies, discrimination against employees, placing employee interests above business interests, health and safety violations, lying to outside stakeholders, retaliation against whistle-blowers, falsifying time reports or hours worked, stealing or theft, delivery of substandard goods or services, and substance abuse at work. That is twelve items. The three areas above are the lecturer's framework; do not mix the two lists.
</Callout>

## Ethical decisions are not always easy

Many ethical decisions are not a choice between good and bad. They are a conflict between two or more important interests. The class uses three dilemmas.

| Dilemma | Facts | The tension |
|---|---|---|
| The small factory | Severe difficulty; all 20 employees may lose their jobs. Management considers retrenching 5 to preserve 15 jobs | Is it right? What alternatives should be investigated first? |
| The supplier | A larger business owes a small local supplier P500,000 and considers delaying payment for six months without telling it | Who should carry the cost of the larger company's survival? |
| Customer data | A struggling company can sell customer information to save jobs, but customers never gave permission | Does a good business outcome justify using information without consent? |

### Two lenses

<KeyTerm title="Outcomes, and principles and rights" tone="purple">
**Outcomes:** which decision is likely to produce the greatest overall benefit or the least harm? Consider who benefits, who is harmed, how seriously, and in the short and long term. **Principles and rights:** are there actions that should not be taken because they violate honesty, fairness, consent, respect or privacy?
</KeyTerm>

Strong analysis uses both. Selling customer data might be defended by outcomes ("it saves jobs") and still violate privacy, consent and trust. The entrepreneur must be able to explain not only what was decided but why.

## Why good people make bad decisions

Unethical behaviour is not always a deliberate decision to become dishonest. Four things push it along.

| Factor | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| Pressure | Meeting sales targets, raising profit, paying debts, keeping a job, keeping the business alive |
| Opportunity | Weak internal controls, nobody checking the records, little supervision, poor accountability |
| Justification | "Everyone does it." "It is only this once." "The business needs to survive." "I deserve it." "Nobody will be harmed." |
| Incentives | A reward system that unintentionally encourages shortcuts |

The deck's example: a manager says anyone who misses P500,000 in sales this month loses their commission, and an employee starts recording sales that are not yet confirmed. Who is responsible: the employee who falsified the information, the manager who created the pressure, or the owner who designed the system? Pressure, opportunity and justification lead to the action. Owners should ask not only "Did the employee reach the target?" but "How was it reached?"

## Integrity in everyday decisions

Integrity shows in ordinary choices: accurate financial and sales records, honest product descriptions and advertising, fair treatment of employees and suppliers, keeping promises to customers, handling mistakes openly, and applying the same standards under pressure.

<Callout type="caution" title="The cash sale test">
A business receives P10,000 in cash. The owner records only P6,000 and keeps P4,000 outside the records. The short-term gain is more cash under the owner's control. The longer-term consequences are inaccurate information, poor management decisions, weak internal controls, loss of trust and compliance problems.
</Callout>

One unethical decision can start a chain reaction: inaccurate information, then wrong management decisions, then loss of trust, then customers or other stakeholders are affected, then reputation is damaged, and the business eventually pays a price.

## Stakeholders

<KeyTerm title="Stakeholder">
A person or group that can affect the performance of the business, or is affected by its performance and decisions.
</KeyTerm>

The chapter treats **owners, customers and employees** as the primary stakeholders because they deal directly with the business. Suppliers, government and communities are also stakeholders.

| Stakeholder | Expects | Integrity means |
|---|---|---|
| Owners | A return on money, time, resources and knowledge: profitability, growth, security, survival | Pursuing profit honestly and protecting the investment |
| Customers | Accurate information, the quality promised, honest advertising, serious handling of complaints, privacy | Caring about customers as individuals, which helps keep their loyalty |
| Employees | Fairness, honesty, impartiality, respect, appropriate working conditions | Fairness in both directions: employees owe honesty, responsible use of resources, confidentiality and no theft or fraud |
| Suppliers | Honest communication, fair dealing, reliable payment, respect for agreements | Remembering that a supplier may be a small business, so delayed payment moves the pressure to it |
| Government | Compliance with laws, regulations, tax and reporting requirements | Avoiding fraudulent reporting, skimming (under-reporting income to cut tax), tax evasion and casual accounting |
| Communities | Employment, products and services, economic activity, tax revenue, local development | Recognising that activities affect people beyond customers and owners |

Interests conflict, and the entrepreneur's task is not to please everyone.

| Decision | Who benefits | Possible trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| Lower prices | Customers | Profit margins may fall |
| Higher wages | Employees | Business costs rise |
| Rapid expansion | Owners and employees | Quality and systems may suffer |
| Delaying supplier payment | The buyer, temporarily | The supplier may face financial trouble |

The entrepreneur recognises the competing interests, understands the consequences, considers fairness and makes responsible trade-offs.

## Four responsibilities of a small business

| Responsibility | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Economic | Be financially viable: cover costs, pay employees, invest and keep operating |
| Legal | Obey laws and regulations. This is a minimum standard |
| Ethical | Avoid dishonest, unfair or harmful practices. An action can be legal and still raise ethical concerns, so ask "Is this right?" as well as "Is this legal?" |
| Discretionary or social | Voluntarily contribute, for example community projects, training, charity or opportunities for disadvantaged groups |

<Callout type="key">
Doing good in one area does not excuse unethical behaviour in another.
</Callout>

## Challenges of acting with integrity

Small businesses face particular pressure when starting, when building a reputation, when moving online and when expanding internationally.

**The legitimacy lie.** A small business creates a false impression to look more established, successful or capable than it is: exaggerating experience, pretending to have more customers, misrepresenting resources or promising what it cannot deliver. The temptation is to win the confidence of customers, investors, suppliers and partners. Trust built on false information is fragile. The chapter suggests building confidence legitimately through the **PRO** factors:

- **P, Product:** show that the product or service meets customer needs.
- **R, Representatives:** make sure the people who represent the business understand the product and can communicate competently.
- **O, Organisation:** show that the organisation will stand behind its product or service.

**The digital marketplace.** Ask how customer information is collected and used, whether online reviews are genuine, whether descriptions and images are accurate, and whether someone else's content or ideas are used fairly. A responsible business does not collect information just because it can: why is it needed, how will it be used, who can see it, and how is it protected?

**Business across borders.** Two views: **ethical imperialism** (the ethical standards of one's own country apply everywhere) and **ethical relativism** (ethical standards depend on local interpretation). Should a business accept a practice because it is common elsewhere? Employees must understand the firm's commitment to integrity, because a one-time unethical practice can become a pattern.

## The integrity edge

Integrity is valuable for moral reasons, and it can also help the business. The lecturer's notes list eight benefits:

1. Improved financial performance.
2. Enhanced brand image and reputation.
3. Increased sales and customer loyalty.
4. Improved productivity and quality.
5. Better recruitment.
6. Reduced employee turnover.
7. Fewer regulatory difficulties.
8. Improved access to capital.

Separately, the Class 2 deck describes how integrity pays as a chain, and trust is the mechanism in it, not one of the eight benefits:

<Callout type="key">
Integrity leads to trust, trust to stronger relationships, relationships to loyalty, loyalty to reputation, and reputation to long-term sustainability. Ethics has both a moral dimension and a business dimension.
</Callout>

The textbook shows its own diagram of these benefits that groups some of them, so when a question asks for the chapter's benefits, use the lecturer's list of eight.

In a small, close market reputation matters even more. Customers share experiences with friends, family and colleagues; word of mouth, WhatsApp and social media spread good and bad experiences quickly. Trust takes years to build and one serious ethical failure can damage it. A good reputation attracts customers; a bad one costs customers, trust, employees and partnerships.

## Building a business with integrity

Integrity should not depend only on the owner's personality. A business needs clear values, leadership, ethical standards, a culture and decision-making processes.

- **Values** such as honesty, respect, truthfulness, fairness and responsibility are meaningful only when they influence behaviour. Writing them on a wall is not enough.
- **Leadership.** The founder's integrity strongly shapes the business. Employees watch what leaders do, reward, tolerate and ignore. An owner who manipulates records but tells staff to be honest sends conflicting messages.
- **Ethical culture.** The chapter gives five principles, each starting with P: **Purpose** (vision and core values guide behaviour), **Pride** (employees who take pride in their work act more responsibly), **Patience** (too much pressure for immediate results encourages shortcuts), **Persistence** (stand by commitments) and **Perspective** (reflect on where the business is going and whether its actions fit its values).
- **A code of ethics** is a set of official standards for employee behaviour. It may cover honesty, conflicts of interest, treatment of customers, confidential information, use of company resources, fraud and theft, relationships with suppliers and reporting unethical behaviour. It works only if employees understand it, are guided on it, see leaders follow it and know the consequences of breaking it.

**Six practical actions** (Class 2): set clear expectations, lead by example, create an environment where concerns can be raised, apply standards consistently, examine incentives that may encourage shortcuts, and learn from mistakes.

## Deciding well: the six-step process

The lecturer's notes, which follow the textbook, and the Class 2 slide (slide 28) cut the six steps in different places. Use the version your question or lecturer asks for, and say which one you are using.

| Step | Notes and textbook | Class 2, slide 28 |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Define the problem: what is happening, why is it ethical, what are the facts? Separate assumptions from facts | Define the ethical problem clearly |
| 2 | Identify alternative solutions: do not assume there are only two | Identify realistic alternatives |
| 3 | Evaluate the alternatives: consequences, stakeholders, fairness, rights, honesty | Evaluate consequences and stakeholder effects |
| 4 | Make the decision: the most responsible option | Consider principles, fairness and rights |
| 5 | Implement the decision: who, what communication, how stakeholders are treated | Make and implement the decision |
| 6 | Evaluate the decision: what happened, were outcomes achieved, who was affected, what was learned | Review the outcome and learn |

Two tests help at step 3:

- **The textbook's four questions:** Is it the truth? Is it fair to all concerned? Will it build goodwill and better friendships? Will it be beneficial to all concerned? The Golden Rule applies as well: treat others as you would want to be treated.
- **The quick ethical test (Class 2, slide 29):** Is it truthful? Is it fair to the people affected? Would I be comfortable explaining the decision publicly? Will it strengthen or damage important relationships? Have I considered alternatives that could reduce harm?

<Callout type="tip" title="Worked case: Kgosi's Catering">
A client asks Kgosi to change the date on an invoice so it can use its remaining annual budget. The contract is a large share of expected revenue, and refusing may affect future business. Step 1: the issue is whether to issue an invoice that does not accurately reflect the transaction. Step 2: agree, refuse, or look for another legitimate way to structure the transaction. Step 3: consider truthfulness, legal implications, effects on the customer and on Kgosi's business, and long-term reputation. Step 4: decide on ethical principles and long-term consequences, not short-term revenue alone. Step 5: communicate the decision professionally and clearly. Step 6: review the effect on trust, reputation, future relationships and standards.
</Callout>

## Social entrepreneurship and the environment

<KeyTerm title="Social entrepreneur">
Someone who develops innovative solutions to society's important needs, problems and opportunities and then makes those solutions happen. Social entrepreneurship has two dimensions: creating positive social or environmental value, and building a viable, sustainable business model. Good intentions are not enough; it must consider customers, revenue, costs, resources and sustainability.
</KeyTerm>

The **triple bottom line** asks three questions of responsible entrepreneurship:

| | Asks |
|---|---|
| People | How does the business affect employees, customers and communities? |
| Profit | Is it financially viable, and can it earn enough to survive and grow? |
| Planet | What are its environmental effects: waste, energy, water, packaging, pollution, disposal of materials? |

Sustainability means protecting and preserving the environment. It can add costs for a small business (waste management, recycling, cleaner production, energy-efficient equipment, responsible packaging) and also create opportunities, including whole new start-ups: recycling, waste collection, reusable products, water conservation, energy efficiency, solar and renewable energy, sustainable agriculture and reducing food waste. The entrepreneurial question is what environmental or social problem exists and whether a viable business can solve it.

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
Treating ethics and integrity as the same thing. Ethics is knowing the standard; integrity is acting on it consistently.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Listing "trust" as one of the eight benefits of integrity. Trust is the link in the chain from integrity to sustainability; the eight benefits are the list in the notes.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Assuming that an action is acceptable because it is legal, or that doing good elsewhere makes up for dishonesty in the core business.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Mixing the two six-step processes in one answer, or inventing examples of misconduct instead of using the lecturer's three areas or the textbook's twelve forms.
</Callout>

## Summary

<Callout type="key">
1. Integrity means consistency between values and behaviour, especially under pressure.
2. Ethical decisions can involve difficult trade-offs and competing stakeholder interests.
3. Pressure, opportunity, incentives and justification increase the risk of unethical behaviour.
4. Leaders shape ethical culture through their actions and systems.
5. Trust and reputation can be valuable assets for small businesses.
6. A structured decision process improves the quality of difficult business decisions.
</Callout>
