## Three routes into business

An entrepreneur does not always develop a business from the beginning. Each route offers a different mix of independence, support, cost and uncertainty.

| Route | What the entrepreneur gets | Main consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Start a new independent business | A new concept, brand and operating system. Full autonomy over product, price and branding | Must build demand and operations from scratch: unproven demand, trial-and-error costs, slow growth |
| Operate a franchise | Permission to use an established brand and, depending on the arrangement, its business system. A proven model, recognised brand, standard systems and training | Pays fees and follows agreed requirements, so autonomy is restricted |
| Buy an existing business | Existing assets or a business with an operating history: functioning assets and multi-year records that reduce market uncertainty | Must verify its condition, value and prospects, and watch for inherited risk such as hidden debts, a poor reputation or bad stock |

An established brand or history is useful evidence, but the entrepreneur still has to check that the opportunity suits their resources, abilities and target market. For example, someone interested in food could build their own takeaway, operate a food franchise or buy an existing restaurant, and should weigh capital, experience, demand and the independence they want.

## What franchising is

<KeyTerm title="Franchising">
A business arrangement in which an owner grants another entrepreneur permission to use its brand and specified business methods under agreed conditions. The **franchisor** grants the rights; the **franchisee** invests in and operates the franchised business. The franchisee usually owns the business, but decisions are subject to the franchise agreement.
</KeyTerm>

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Franchise agreement | The contract setting out rights, responsibilities and conditions (territory, fees, quality standards, termination) |
| Initial franchise fee | The payment required to enter the system |
| Royalty | A continuing payment to the franchisor, often a percentage of sales |
| Advertising contribution | A payment towards agreed advertising or marketing |
| Territory | The area in which the franchisee may operate |
| Operations manual | The franchise's operating procedures and standards |
| Franchise disclosure document | Information about the franchisor and the opportunity, including costs, restrictions and risks |

<Callout type="caution" title="The fee is not the investment">
The initial franchise fee is only one part of the investment. The franchisee may also need premises, equipment, inventory, staff and working capital.
</Callout>

## Franchising arrangements

Some arrangements describe the business system being licensed, others describe ownership, expansion or location, so one franchise can fit more than one category.

| Arrangement | What it is |
|---|---|
| Product and trade-name franchising | Permission to sell specified products under the franchisor's name or trademark, for example fuel stations or authorised dealers. Control over the whole operation is usually less extensive |
| Business-format franchising | A broader system: brand identity, operating procedures, staff training, marketing support, service standards, purchasing arrangements and reporting systems. Two Men and a Truck is an example |
| Multiple-unit ownership | One franchisee owns more than one outlet in the system |
| Area developer | Rights to develop several outlets in an agreed area, usually to a schedule |
| Master licensee | Rights over a larger territory, possibly including recruiting or supporting other franchisees, depending on the agreement |
| Piggyback franchising | A franchise operates inside another business's premises |
| Co-branding | Two or more brands operate together at one location or in a combined offering |
| Multibrand franchising | An owner runs franchises of different brands |

## Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | But remember |
|---|---|
| Access to an established brand | Recognition does not guarantee demand in a particular location |
| An established operating system (ordering, stock control, service, records, staff) | The franchisee still faces risks in location, competition, cash flow, staff and demand |
| Training and continuing support | If support is weak the franchisee may struggle while still paying fees |
| Marketing and purchasing support across the network | Assess the real costs and benefits |
| Reduced uncertainty in some areas | An established system is evidence, not a guarantee |

| Disadvantages and risks | Detail |
|---|---|
| Substantial costs | A large initial investment and continuing payments. Revenue-based royalties are due even when the outlet makes little profit or a loss |
| Restrictions on decisions | The franchisor may control location, products, suppliers, appearance, advertising and opening hours, which can limit responding to local conditions |
| Dependence on franchisor support | Training, systems, products or marketing may depend on the franchisor |
| Shared reputation | Poor service at one outlet can affect the whole brand |
| Territorial and contractual concerns | **Encroachment** (a new outlet or channel close enough to hurt an existing franchisee), **churning** (terminating and reselling an operation to collect more fees), supplier conflicts, unbalanced contract terms, and renewal and exit restrictions |

These are matters to investigate, not problems every franchise has.

## Understanding franchise costs

A prospective franchisee must estimate the complete commitment. The class slides name five categories: the initial franchise fee, investment costs (premises, equipment, fit-out, vehicles, signage, opening stock), the royalty fee, the advertising fee and working capital. The slides give typical ranges of **4% to 8%** of gross revenue for royalties and **1% to 3%** for the advertising contribution.

<Steps title="Worked example: what the franchise fees leave you">
  <Step title="State the figures">
    A hypothetical franchise has monthly revenue of P100,000, a royalty of 6% and an advertising contribution of 1%.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Royalty">
    P100,000 x 6% = P6,000.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Advertising contribution">
    P100,000 x 1% = P1,000.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Combined payments">
    P6,000 + P1,000 = P7,000.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What remains">
    P100,000 - P7,000 = P93,000. This is not profit: it must still cover stock or materials, wages, rent, utilities and other expenses.
  </Step>
</Steps>

A franchise evaluation therefore examines both profitability and the timing of cash receipts and payments.

## Evaluating a franchise: seven steps

The class slides and the student notes describe the same seven steps in slightly different words.

| Step | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1. Assess personal suitability (self-assessment) | Do I understand the industry, will I follow standard procedures, can I manage daily operations, do I have the capital, how much risk can I carry? Someone who values freedom over product and branding may find a tightly controlled franchise hard |
| 2. Investigate the market | The proposed location and demand: target market, competitors, accessibility, spending patterns and likely sales. A successful outlet elsewhere does not prove local demand |
| 3. Investigate the franchisor | Experience, history, support capability, network, track record, financial stability and litigation history, and how it handles problems |
| 4. Speak to existing and former franchisees | Were start-up costs close to the estimates, was the training useful, what support continues, how long to stable cash flow, what difficulties occurred, why did former franchisees leave? Use several sources, not one favourable account |
| 5. Examine the financial requirements | Total investment, continuing fees, operating costs and working capital; realistic projections, and what happens if sales are lower or costs higher. The slides suggest including about six months of working capital |
| 6. Review the agreement and documents | Territory, suppliers, fees, standards, renewal, termination and sale, including the disclosure document. The slides say the disclosure document covers 23 areas such as fees, litigation and turnover; requirements differ between jurisdictions, so separate the general purpose of disclosure from the rules for a particular deal |
| 7. Obtain independent review and decide | Accountants and qualified advisers check the financial assumptions and contract. The decision should rest on evidence and the entrepreneur's ability to operate it |

## Becoming a franchisor

A business owner may use franchising to expand through other entrepreneurs, but the original business must have a system others can reproduce. Five considerations: a **reproducible model**, **financial considerations** that work for both sides, the **assistance** franchisees need, a clear **operations manual**, and **long-term value** beyond the first agreement. A business that depends on its founder's personal talent may be hard to franchise until that knowledge is teachable and its operations repeatable.

## Buying an existing business

### Four reasons to buy

1. **Reducing uncertainties.** An operating history gives records on sales, costs and customer behaviour. It is evidence, though future performance may change.
2. **Acquiring ongoing operations and relationships.** Equipment, experienced staff and customer and supplier relationships, though the buyer must check which will continue after the change of ownership.
3. **A favourable price.** It may cost less than building a comparable business, but a low price can reflect declining demand, damaged equipment or liabilities, so find out why.
4. **Faster entry.** The buyer may begin trading sooner, depending on the business's condition and on premises and contracts continuing.

### Finding a business

Before searching, decide your interests and experience, talents and management skills, preferred location and size, available investment, acceptable risk and the time you can commit, so you buy to suit these criteria rather than because a business is available. Brokers can introduce buyers and sellers, but the buyer must still evaluate independently.

### Due diligence

<KeyTerm title="Due diligence">
The systematic investigation of a business opportunity before committing to it. It helps the buyer verify the seller's claims, identify obligations and decide whether the business is worth the price.
</KeyTerm>

| Area | What to check |
|---|---|
| Financial | Statements, tax records, sales records, bank statements, receivables, payables and borrowings, over several years (the slides say three to five) to see trends. Are sales supported by evidence, is it profitable, does profit turn into cash, are customers paying on time, are significant expenses missing, does it rely on a few customers? The slides flag a customer holding more than 20% of sales |
| Operational | Equipment, stock, premises, systems and staff capabilities: repairs, replacement, obsolescence, owner dependence, and the length and assignment terms of the lease |
| Customers, suppliers, market | Customer retention, supplier reliability, competition and future demand. Do relationships belong to the business or to the seller's personal connections? |
| Contracts and obligations | Leases, customer agreements, supplier arrangements and other commitments, and which can continue after the purchase |
| Reasons for sale | Retirement, change of interests, financial pressure or weakening demand; check the explanation against the records |

<Callout type="caution" title="Profit is not cash">
A credit sale can add to reported profit before the customer pays, so a profitable business can still run short of cash, especially during a change of ownership.
</Callout>

### Valuing the business

The asking price is what the seller wants. Valuation estimates what the business may be worth from evidence and stated assumptions.

| Approach | Main focus | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Asset-based | Net asset value: fair value of assets less relevant liabilities | May overlook earning capacity and goodwill |
| Market-comparable | Prices paid for similar businesses, adjusted for size, location, profitability, assets and risk | Truly comparable deals can be hard to find |
| Cash-flow-based | The cash the business is expected to generate and the return the investor needs | Sensitive to forecasts and risk assumptions |

Example of the asset approach: if relevant assets have a fair value of P500,000 and liabilities are P180,000, the net asset value is P500,000 - P180,000 = P320,000. Which liabilities count depends on what is being acquired. Using more than one approach helps explain why estimates differ.

Financial records alone do not explain future prospects. Also ask whether the location is improving or declining, whether the reputation is strong, whether customers and key employees will stay, whether new competitors will affect demand, and whether the business needs major operational improvement. A business with good past results may still face weakening prospects.

### Negotiating and closing

After investigation and valuation the parties negotiate what the buyer receives, the price, payment arrangements and how outstanding matters are handled. In an **asset purchase** the buyer acquires specified assets; in an **entity purchase** the buyer acquires ownership interests in the existing entity. The structure affects what transfers and which obligations remain, so do not assume every purchase works the same way. The slides list four closing steps: a **bill of sale** formally transferring the assets, **tax clearances** confirming no tax obligations pass to the buyer, **lease assignment** with the landlord's consent, and **payment escrow** holding the funds until conditions are met. Professional help should make sure the deal reflects the agreed terms.

## Case: Two Men and a Truck

The case shows business-format franchising at work.

| Case feature | Chapter concept |
|---|---|
| A common brand (the stick men logo) | The franchisor and franchisee relationship |
| A two-week training course and training in moving techniques and business management | Business-format franchising |
| An initial fee of $45,000 (or $85,000 if the franchisee previously operated in the area) and total start-up costs of $158,000 to $460,910 | Total investment, not just the fee |
| A royalty of 6% of gross revenue plus 1% for advertising | Continuing franchise costs |
| Territories based on a population of generally 250,000 to 420,000 | Territorial arrangements |
| Monthly reports, a toll-free support line, annual meeting, branded materials and a system-wide purchasing system | Continuing support and purchasing benefits |

The company began in the early 1980s as two brothers using an old pickup truck, and Mary Ellen Sheets bought a 14-foot truck for $350 in 1985. Treat the figures as evidence from a historical case, not as a statement of the company's current terms. When analysing a case, explain why a feature matters: practical training helps maintain service standards, while continuing fees affect an outlet's financial performance.

## Supplementary concepts

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Independent contracting | Providing an agreed service while operating independently | A freelance photographer completing a client assignment |
| Gig economy | Work organised around individual tasks or short engagements, often through platforms | A designer completing separate projects for different clients |
| Sharing economy | Temporary access to assets or resources, often through platforms | Offering a spare room for short stays |

They can overlap, and promoting a business online does not by itself make it part of the sharing economy; the nature of the activity matters.

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
Treating the initial franchise fee as the whole investment, or treating revenue after fees (P93,000 in the example) as profit.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Assuming every franchise has encroachment, churning or unfair terms, or assuming none does. They are things to investigate.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Buying a business because it is cheap or available without finding out why the price is low, or relying on a single year of profit instead of several years of records.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Confusing profit with cash flow, or using one valuation method and treating its answer as the value.
</Callout>

## Summary

<Callout type="key">
Franchising gives access to an established brand and system, alongside costs and restrictions, and a suitable franchise needs careful investigation of the franchisor, the market, the finances and the agreement. Buying an existing business can reduce some uncertainties, provide ongoing operations, offer a favourable price and allow quicker entry, and the buyer must verify these benefits through due diligence and valuation. In both routes, decide on verified evidence, realistic financial expectations and personal suitability.
</Callout>
