## What feasibility analysis is

<KeyTerm title="Feasibility analysis">
A preliminary investigation of whether a business idea is practical and potentially viable. It examines the conditions under which the venture could work, identifies the important uncertainties and helps the entrepreneur decide what to do next.
</KeyTerm>

Feasibility is conditional. A venture might work in a small collection area at one price and fail when it serves customers spread across a whole city at the same price. So describe the proposed version of the business precisely: its customers, location, offering, price and delivery method.

### The four areas

| Area | Main issues | Decision question |
|---|---|---|
| Product or service | Desirability and demand | Does the offering solve a valued problem and attract enough paying customers? |
| Industry and target market | Industry attractiveness and target market attractiveness | Is the business environment favourable, and is the chosen segment worth serving? |
| Organizational | Management prowess and resource sufficiency | Can the founder or team obtain and coordinate what is needed to deliver? |
| Financial | Start-up cash, comparable businesses and overall financial attractiveness | Can the venture get the cash it needs and produce acceptable results? |

<Callout type="key">
The four areas are connected. Strong customer interest cannot make up for a service that cannot be delivered, and a capable team cannot sustain a business whose price does not cover its costs. The recommendation must weigh the evidence from all four.
</Callout>

### When and why

Do the analysis early, after an idea appears and before big commitments such as buying specialised equipment, signing a long lease or hiring a full team. It is not done once: a new supplier quotation, a competitor's reaction or a change of target customer can send the entrepreneur back to reassess. There are four main reasons to do it.

1. **It tests assumptions.** A founder may believe customers want speed, then find that reliability matters more or that the price is above their budget.
2. **It finds resource gaps.** A promising opportunity may need transport, a facility, technical knowledge or a partner the founder cannot yet reach.
3. **It makes better use of money and time.** The result may be a smaller pilot, rented equipment or a decision to stop. Stopping an unsuitable idea is a sound entrepreneurial decision.
4. **It improves later planning.** Evidence about customers, competitor prices, supplier terms and capacity gives the business model, the marketing and operating plans and the projections a firmer base.

<Callout type="caution" title="It reduces uncertainty, not risk to zero">
Research can be incomplete, customer behaviour can change and execution can fall short. A favourable conclusion should state its evidence, its limits and the conditions still to be met.
</Callout>

| Document | Main purpose | Typical output |
|---|---|---|
| Feasibility analysis | Whether, and under what conditions, to pursue an idea | Evidence, uncertainties and a recommendation |
| Business model | How the venture creates, delivers and captures value | Customers, value proposition, activities, resources and revenue logic |
| Business plan | How the venture will operate, compete and develop | Integrated plans and financial projections |

A business plan can contain feasibility evidence, but writing a convincing plan does not make its assumptions true.

### How to run the investigation

<Steps title="Six steps">
  <Step title="Define the business and its customers">
    State the version of the idea you are testing.
  </Step>
  <Step title="List the assumptions that must be true">
    Everything the venture relies on, from customer behaviour to supplier terms.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Find the assumptions that would sink it if false">
    These are researched first.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Choose methods and collect evidence">
    Match each method to the uncertainty it can settle.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Interpret the findings">
    Include contradictory evidence and limitations.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Recommend a next step">
    Proceed to further development, revise and retest, or discontinue.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Keep **facts**, **assumptions** and **forecasts** apart. A dated supplier quotation is evidence of an offered price, though its terms may expire. A claim that customers will buy weekly is an assumption until investigated. A monthly sales estimate is a forecast built from assumptions and evidence.

## Product or service feasibility

This area judges the offering from the customer's side: are the benefits appealing, and is there a credible basis for enough demand? Keep the two issues distinct in an exam answer.

<KeyTerm title="Desirability and demand">
**Desirability** is the appeal and usefulness of the offering to intended customers. **Demand** is their willingness and ability to buy it under specified conditions of price, frequency and channel.
</KeyTerm>

### Desirability

Begin with the customer's problem, what they do now and what dissatisfies them. Ask how serious the problem is, how often it occurs and what improvement the offering makes. An offering creates value by cutting cost, saving time, improving reliability, adding convenience or making a hard activity possible. A feature matters only when it produces a benefit the customer values: WhatsApp ordering is a feature, and arranging collection without travelling to a shop is the possible benefit. Timing matters too, because customers who are away or busy with other priorities will show little interest in a useful idea.

A **concept statement** is a short description of the proposed business used to get feedback: the offering, the customers, the benefits, what is distinctive and who will deliver it, with a price and key terms when feedback needs them. A **concept test** shows the statement, a demonstration or a prototype to intended customers and asks what appeals, what is unclear, what worries them and how it compares with what they use now. A **prototype** is an early version for learning, such as a trial booking process or a manually delivered service, and it should be built to investigate an uncertainty, not to flatter the idea.

The lecturer's classroom example: Campus Laundry Collect would collect standard laundry orders from students in selected areas near UB, take bookings through WhatsApp, return the laundry within 48 hours at P60 per defined standard order, and use a partner laundry for the washing. The statement must say what a standard order includes so every respondent judges the same service.

### Demand

Customers may like an idea and keep using their current alternative. Look for a credible path from interest to purchases and repeat use, and test willingness to pay alongside ability to pay. In business markets the user may differ from the person who approves the purchase. A low introductory price can attract trial orders that vanish at the normal price, so a forecast should state the price, the segment, the purchase frequency and the repeat-purchase assumption.

| Method | What it can show | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Customer interviews | Problems, current behaviour, preferences, reasons for switching | A small or biased sample may miss other needs |
| Concept test or prototype | Reactions to the benefits, design and process | Positive feedback may not lead to purchases |
| Buying-intentions survey | Stated likelihood of buying at a defined price | People may overstate what they will do |
| Observation and field research | How customers solve the problem now | The reasons behind behaviour may still need investigation |
| Paid pilot | Actual purchases, delivery experience and repeat use | A limited trial may not represent demand at scale |
| Secondary research | Population, spending, sector trends, existing alternatives | Published data may not match the intended segment |

Practical field investigation (visiting competing outlets, comparing their offers and talking to prospective customers) is sometimes called gumshoe research.

**Ask useful questions.** Start with recent behaviour: how did you handle your laundry last week, what did it cost, what was inconvenient? Only then show the concept and ask about it at the proposed price. Avoid leading questions such as "Would you support this excellent student business?", and do not treat friends' encouragement as market evidence. Recruit people who fit the customer profile and record the sample and method. A page view, a like, a request for information and a paid order are four different observations.

<Steps title="Worked example: reading a small survey">
  <Step title="The result">
    Twenty acquaintances answer. Twelve like the idea and eight would consider paying P60.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What it shows">
    Some initial appeal and some stated intention to buy. Eight of twenty is 40% of this sample.
  </Step>
  <Step title="What it does not show">
    It does not give eight regular customers, and 40% cannot be applied to all UB students. The sample is small and may be unrepresentative, and "would consider paying" says nothing about when or how often.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The next step">
    Test a clearly defined offer with intended customers and record actual orders, cancellations, repeat purchases and feedback.
  </Step>
</Steps>

A good judgement names the benefit that is supported, the evidence about purchases and what is still uncertain. For example: the service shows initial appeal because respondents value collection, but regular demand at P60 is untested, so a paid pilot is needed before assuming monthly sales will cover costs. That is stronger than "it is feasible because many people like it".

## Industry and target market feasibility

This area asks whether the sector offers a reasonable opportunity and whether the chosen segment is reachable and worth serving. An **industry** is the group of businesses offering similar or related goods and services; laundry businesses sit in a wider laundry and cleaning sector. A **target market** is the particular group the venture chooses to serve, such as students in selected accommodation near UB who want paid collection and return. Assess the two separately.

### Industry attractiveness

An attractive industry lets a well-designed venture compete and earn acceptable returns. Look at demand trends, competitors and substitutes, suppliers, how easy it is to enter and what may change. Many customers help only if the business can serve them at sustainable cost and price.

- **Growth** can open room for a newcomer and also attract rivals. A new sector may have unmet needs and also uncertainty about technology, behaviour or rules. A mature industry can still reward a venture that serves a neglected segment or is more reliable. Age alone does not decide feasibility.
- **Structure** matters. A fragmented industry has many small firms and may leave room for a local venture. A concentrated one is dominated by a few large firms, which can make access to customers, suppliers or distribution harder. Check the actual conditions.
- **Competition and entry barriers** shape prices and margins. If customers compare offers and switch easily, prices come under pressure. Barriers protect established firms; low barriers make launch easy and also let rivals copy a good idea.
- **Substitutes** meet the same need another way. For laundry collection they include washing at home, using a machine in the accommodation or asking someone else. The venture must know why customers would choose it instead.

The five competitive forces are a prompt for questions: rivalry among existing firms, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, supplier bargaining power and buyer bargaining power. At this stage use them to find what needs research, not to write a long strategy. For the laundry service, a single partner with limited capacity may hold a lot of bargaining power, and customers with several convenient alternatives hold some too, which affects reliability and the price the venture can charge.

### Target market attractiveness

A target market is attractive when enough suitable customers can be reached and served, have the need and can pay a sustainable price. Define the segment by what affects buying and delivery: location, income or budget, behaviour, type of organisation or the situation in which the problem occurs. "Everyone who needs laundry" is too broad to plan a first venture; a manageable collection area, a kind of customer, the need and the offer is workable. A niche can simplify communication and delivery, though larger competitors may still react.

Investigate segment size, buying frequency, affordability, access and willingness to switch, and whether demand is seasonal (a student service differs in term and vacation, so one busy week is not year-round demand). Separate **total market size** from **attainable sales**: a small business has limited awareness, capacity and reach, so forecast the customers it can really win and keep.

<Steps title="Worked example: a market estimate">
  <Step title="The assumptions">
    A founder finds 300 potentially suitable students in a defined collection area. Assume 10% buy, and each buyer places two orders a month.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The forecast">
    300 × 10% = 30 customers, and 30 × 2 = 60 orders a month.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Compare it with what is needed">
    If the venture needs 100 monthly orders to cover its operating costs, 60 orders is a financial concern.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Respond properly">
    Do not raise the assumed purchase rate just to reach break-even. The rate and the frequency are assumptions that need evidence, and the sum does not validate them. Investigate the segment, test acquisition and repeat use, or change the operating model.
  </Step>
</Steps>

To research the industry and segment, compare competitors' prices, opening times, terms, locations and customer experience, with dates, and separate direct rivals from substitutes. Talk to customers and suppliers, and use reliable published information, such as official national statistics, only when it matches the research question, checking each source's date, coverage and definitions. A good judgement keeps the questions open: existing laundries show established alternatives whose prices and collection services are unknown, and students in the proposed area may be reachable, but enough demand at P60 is unverified.

## Organizational feasibility

This area asks whether the founder or team can organise and deliver the business: management ability and access to essential nonfinancial resources.

### Management prowess

<KeyTerm title="Management prowess">
The practical ability of the founder or team to launch and manage the venture.
</KeyTerm>

Passion and persistence matter, but they must come with an understanding of the market and sound decisions. Assess relevant experience, customer knowledge, technical competence, financial understanding, communication and the ability to coordinate work. A founder may make a good product and lack experience in pricing, buying or handling complaints, so record strengths and gaps. For the laundry service, Lebo's customer service experience helps with bookings and problems, but the founder must also schedule collections, manage the partner, keep records, watch costs and handle lost or damaged garments. A 48-hour promise creates duties across the whole process.

A team can combine skills. Where a capability is missing the options are to recruit, train, use an adviser or contract a specialist, and the analysis should say who does the essential work and whether they are free when needed. **Time is part of capability:** an operating plan that needs the founder every afternoon is unreliable if they have classes at collection time.

### Resource sufficiency

<KeyTerm title="Resource sufficiency">
Access to the nonfinancial resources needed to deliver the offer: premises, equipment, skilled staff, suppliers, service partners, transport, distribution channels, systems and knowledge. Access can come through ownership, rental, partnership or contracting.
</KeyTerm>

List the resources the proposed version needs and check their availability, suitability and dependability. An unconfirmed verbal promise gives less assurance than a documented arrangement with clear capacity and service terms. Ask what happens if a resource disappears.

| Resource for the laundry service | Evidence to obtain | Effect of a gap |
|---|---|---|
| Laundry partner | Confirmed capacity, quality expectations and turnaround terms | Orders may be late or inconsistent |
| Collection and return transport | An available vehicle or dependable arrangement | Cost or delay may undermine the promise |
| Garment tracking | A tested recording and identification process | Orders may be mixed up, lost or returned wrongly |
| Customer communication | A working booking process with named responsibility | Missed bookings and unresolved complaints |
| Founder and team time | A realistic weekly schedule with assigned roles | The service may stop when the founder is unavailable |

**Capacity** is the volume the business can handle at its promised standard. If a partner can reliably handle only 70 orders a month, a plan that needs 100 requires more investigation. Capacity estimates should allow for peaks, collection schedules and the time to fix problems. Watch for heavy dependence on one person or supplier, and for backup. Outsourcing saves owning equipment but not coordinating quality, cost and promises. Where permissions, food safety or customer information apply, find out which current requirements apply before promising delivery, which is research, not an assumption that every venture faces the same rules.

<Callout type="note" title="One issue, two areas">
Access to transport belongs in the organizational analysis, and its cost belongs in the financial estimates. A single issue can affect more than one area.
</Callout>

A useful conclusion says what is available, what is missing and how the gap affects launch: Lebo has relevant customer service experience and a smartphone, but transport and partner capacity are not confirmed, so these should be verified and the tracking process tested before accepting a larger volume.

## Financial feasibility

Financial feasibility is a preliminary assessment of whether the venture can meet its cash requirements and produce acceptable results. It has three central issues, and the calculations below support them without replacing customer and operational evidence.

| Issue | What to establish |
|---|---|
| Total start-up cash needed | How much cash is needed before and after opening, and where it will come from |
| Financial performance of similar businesses | Whether the estimates are realistic against comparable firms |
| Overall financial attractiveness | Whether profit, cash flow, growth, risk and return justify the commitment |

### Start-up cash

Start-up cash covers what is needed to be ready for the first sale: equipment, opening inventory, deposits, setup work and costs before revenue starts. Use quotations and published charges, not guesses. Also estimate the cash needed after opening, because suppliers, staff and rent may be due before customer receipts arrive. This opening operating buffer is a cash planning estimate, not the accounting measure of net working capital (current assets less current liabilities). Prepare a cash requirements schedule and name the source of each requirement, separating funds already held from loans, grants or investment that are only proposed.

| Requirement | Amount |
|---|---|
| Setup costs before the first sale | P3,000 |
| Proposed opening operating buffer | P2,000 |
| Total initial cash requirement | P5,000 |
| Lebo's available savings | P5,000 |
| Difference | P0 |

P3,000 + P2,000 = P5,000, and P5,000 − P5,000 = P0. Matching savings do not prove the funding is enough: the buffer must be tested against payment timing and lower sales, and a missing cost, a dearer partner price or a longer wait for receipts could still cause a shortfall. Avoid double counting, for instance adding stock again when it is already in the setup costs.

### Similar businesses

Comparable businesses give a reference for judging whether estimates are realistic: their prices, sales patterns, cost categories, margins, seasonality and payment terms, from public reports, industry information, supplier talks and voluntary conversations with owners. Choose truly comparable firms: a laundry that owns its machines has a different cost structure from a collection service that contracts out the washing, and a long-established outlet has a customer base a newcomer must build. An advertised price shows the offer, not the profit, and public accounts may cover several sites. Record the source, period and differences. Use benchmarks to challenge your assumptions: a much higher margin than comparable firms needs an explanation or a revised forecast, and copying a rival's sales estimate ignores the new venture's own capacity and reach.

### Overall attractiveness

Attractiveness combines profit potential, cash flow, growth, risk and returns: can the business cover its costs, can sales be forecast credibly, will customers buy again, and can the cash it generates support operations and development? The return that is acceptable depends on the entrepreneur's aims. A small owner-managed venture may want a sustainable income and manageable risk, while an outside investor may also need a realistic exit route such as dividends or a later sale of their stake; that is not a requirement that every small business plan a stock exchange listing. Growth has a cost: more orders may need more transport, staff or equipment, so revenue can rise while the cash balance comes under pressure.

### Revenue and cost behaviour

<KeyTerm title="Contribution">
Selling price less variable cost per unit. Each sale contributes this amount towards fixed costs and, once they are covered, towards an operating surplus. Contribution is not net profit.
</KeyTerm>

Revenue is the value of sales; for one standard service it is orders multiplied by the price per order, so define the unit (an order, a kilogram or a garment) before calculating. A **variable cost** changes with activity, such as a washing charge for each order. A **fixed cost** stays the same within the relevant range and period, such as an agreed monthly rent, and may step up if the venture outgrows its capacity. The laundry example assumes a price of P60, a variable cost of P40 per standard order (which already includes washing, collection and packaging, so none is added again) and fixed monthly costs of P2,000.

<Steps title="Worked example: contribution and break-even">
  <Step title="Contribution per order">
    P60 − P40 = P20. Each extra order contributes P20 towards the fixed costs.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Operating break-even volume">
    Fixed costs divided by contribution: P2,000 ÷ P20 = 100 orders a month. If the answer is not a whole number, round up to the next saleable unit.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Revenue at break-even">
    100 orders × P60 = P6,000 of sales.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Read it carefully">
    The formula assumes the price, the variable cost per order and the fixed costs hold over the range considered. With zero or negative contribution it gives no useful positive volume, so the price or cost structure has to change.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Operating result = (orders × P20) − P2,000.

| Orders a month | Revenue | Variable costs | Contribution | Operating result after P2,000 fixed costs |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 80 | P4,800 | P3,200 | P1,600 | P400 loss |
| 100 | P6,000 | P4,000 | P2,000 | P0 |
| 120 | P7,200 | P4,800 | P2,400 | P400 surplus |

A forecast of 80 orders gives a P400 monthly operating loss, so the model needs revising or evidence of higher sustainable volume. It would be wrong to assume 120 orders only because that produces a surplus.

<Callout type="caution" title="Break-even is not payback">
Monthly operating break-even does not mean the P3,000 setup spending has been recovered; that needs further cash generated over time. The example also leaves out tax, finance costs and owner pay unless the stated costs include them.
</Callout>

### Profit and cash flow

Profit is revenue less expenses for an accounting period. Cash flow records when money is actually received and paid. Credit sales create revenue before cash arrives, and equipment purchases, loan repayments and owner withdrawals affect cash differently from expenses.

<Steps title="Worked example: a surplus with a cash shortage">
  <Step title="The month">
    A separate fictional month has 120 completed orders. Revenue is P7,200 and operating payments are P4,800 variable plus P2,000 fixed, P6,800 in all.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The profit view">
    P7,200 − P6,800 = P400 operating surplus.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The cash view">
    Only P3,600 is collected that month, so cash movement is P3,600 − P6,800 = negative P3,200.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The gap">
    The other P3,600 (P7,200 − P3,600) is still owed by customers. A positive operating result sits beside a serious cash requirement.
  </Step>
</Steps>

Prepare a cash forecast with the opening balance, receipts, payments and closing balance for each period, including start-up spending and repayment timing, and do not treat revenue as receipts unless the payment terms support it.

### Sensitivity analysis

<KeyTerm title="Sensitivity analysis">
Examining how results change when one assumption changes, to find which uncertain figures matter most.
</KeyTerm>

| Scenario | Contribution per order | Monthly break-even volume |
|---|---|---|
| Base case: price P60, variable cost P40 | P20 | 100 orders |
| Price falls to P55, variable cost stays P40 | P15 | 134 orders (133.33 rounded up) |
| Variable cost rises to P45, price stays P60 | P15 | 134 orders (133.33 rounded up) |
| Fixed costs rise to P2,500, contribution stays P20 | P20 | 125 orders |

The lower price raises the number of orders needed even though it might lift demand. Whether the trade-off helps depends on real customer behaviour and delivery capacity, so evaluate the linked effects instead of assuming a discount helps.

A sound financial conclusion combines cash requirements, credible benchmarks, operating results and risk, and names the assumptions that matter and the missing evidence. For Lebo the untested 80-order forecast is below break-even, and the buffer and costs need validating; matching savings to a P5,000 requirement does not settle either.

## Feasibility templates

A feasibility template is a structured set of questions or fields that makes an entrepreneur examine an idea consistently, record evidence and spot gaps. It helps compare ideas and explain a recommendation. Barringer's **First Screen** is an early screening tool whose topics cover the strength of the idea, industry issues, target market and customer issues, founder issues and financial issues; they feed the broader four-area investigation. Use the prescribed edition for its exact questions and scoring. Filling the boxes helps only when the answers rest on evidence.

Use a template when first assessing an idea, when choosing between several, and before committing substantial resources, and again when the offering, customers, supplier terms or costs change. Match the detail to the commitment: a short screen and a research plan may do for an early discussion, while expensive equipment or significant finance needs stronger evidence and fuller estimates.

The lecturer's own classroom adaptation has these fields, repeated for each important issue in all four areas.

| Field | What to record |
|---|---|
| Business concept | Offering, customers, location, price and delivery method |
| Feasibility area and issue | The particular question being investigated |
| Finding | What the evidence currently suggests |
| Supporting evidence | Source, date, method and result |
| Assumption or missing evidence | What has not yet been established |
| Assessment | Promising, concern or insufficient evidence |
| Next action | The research, test or resource arrangement needed |
| Overall recommendation | Proceed to further development, revise and retest, or discontinue, with reasons |

A completed demand entry for the laundry service: the issue is demand at P60 per standard order; the finding is some stated interest but untested paid demand; the evidence is a fictional survey of 20 acquaintances, 8 of whom would consider paying; the limitation is a small convenience sample and no completed orders; the assessment is **insufficient evidence**; the next action is a limited paid trial with intended customers, recording repeat use. "Insufficient evidence" means the question is unanswered, not that the business will fail.

Set decision criteria before testing where possible (what delivery reliability would be acceptable, what evidence would support the sales and cost assumptions) and do not change them just to declare success. A promising assessment justifies the next stage, a concern may call for a change of price, segment, scale, partner or delivery method, and a serious unresolved condition can justify discontinuing the idea in its present form. Do not rely on an average rating: good feedback cannot make up for an unavailable essential resource, and a low setup cost cannot make up for continuing losses.

## Integrated case: Campus Laundry Collect

Lebo proposes a WhatsApp laundry collection and return service for students in selected areas near UB, with a 48-hour turnaround at P60 per standard order. An informal survey of 20 acquaintances gave 12 positive reactions and 8 who would consider paying, and there are no paid orders. Two laundries operate nearby but Lebo has not compared their prices or collection services. A potential partner gave a preliminary quotation used for the P40 variable cost; capacity and terms are unconfirmed. Lebo has a smartphone and customer service experience, but reliable transport and garment tracking are missing. Lebo forecasts 80 monthly orders without sales evidence. Fixed costs are P2,000 a month, setup costs P3,000, the proposed buffer P2,000 and savings P5,000. All of this is fictional.

| Area | What the evidence says | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| Product or service | Initial appeal and stated interest; regular paid demand unverified | Test a defined offer at the intended price with target customers |
| Industry and market | Rivals and alternatives exist; segment appeal and achievable sales uncertain | Compare alternatives and investigate a manageable collection area |
| Organizational | Some customer service capability; essential delivery resources unconfirmed | Verify partner capacity, transport and garment tracking |
| Financial | Contribution P20 and break-even 100 orders; 80 orders gives a P400 loss | Validate costs, cash timing and realistic sales scenarios |

**Recommendation: revise and retest.** There is not yet evidence of regular paying demand, essential delivery arrangements are unresolved and the forecast is below operating break-even. These concerns are material because they affect both the promise to customers and the ability to cover costs. Lebo could study a smaller collection area, agree clear partner terms and run a limited service with reliable transport and a simple tracking system, recording demand at the stated price, repeat use, delivery performance and actual costs, and then reassess.

## Research and testing in practice

- **Turn assumptions into testable questions.** "Students need the service" is too broad. "Students in the selected area will place repeat orders at P60 under the stated collection terms" is a hypothesis that evidence can contradict.
- **Match the test to the uncertainty.** Interviews explore a problem and its causes, a booking prototype shows usability problems, a paid pilot shows purchase behaviour and delivery cost, and a partner trial shows operating capacity. One method rarely settles everything.
- **What recent research suggests.** The student notes cite Camuffo and colleagues (2024), a study of four randomized controlled trials with 759 firms, in which a scientific approach to entrepreneurial decisions led to more ideas being stopped and a more selective pattern of big strategic changes. The teaching point is that learning an idea should stop or change is a useful result. It does not promise profit for any particular Botswana venture.
- **Record evidence responsibly.** Note who took part, when, how they were chosen, what was asked and what was seen; get agreement to take part and collect only what is needed; never invent responses or quotations. Five interviews are useful exploration but give no population purchase rate, a pilot with close friends may not predict strangers' demand, and a result at a subsidised price may not hold at the commercial price.
- **Digital tools and AI** can organise surveys, compare scenarios and draft a research plan, but the student must check that the questions are neutral and relevant. Generated customer answers, invented market statistics and fabricated supplier quotations are not evidence. Open the underlying source of any online figure and check its date, geography, definitions and method; a national population total does not show how many customers a small venture can reach.

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
Treating enthusiasm, compliments or social media likes as proof of paid demand.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Defining the target market so broadly that research and delivery become unrealistic, or ignoring substitutes because they solve the problem in a different form.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Assuming a growing industry guarantees success, or treating a resource as confirmed when it rests on an untested promise.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Forecasting sales with no evidence about winning customers and repeat use, then adjusting the assumptions until the venture breaks even.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Adding operating costs twice or leaving out costs that matter, and confusing contribution with profit, profit with cash, or operating break-even with recovering the start-up investment.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Reading every unknown as a good finding, or as a reason to reject the idea. "Insufficient evidence" means the question is still open.
</Callout>

## Summary

<Callout type="key">
Feasibility analysis is an early, evidence-based check of whether an idea can work, covering product or service, industry and target market, organizational and financial feasibility. Separate facts from assumptions and forecasts, test assumptions with methods that match the uncertainty, and weigh all four areas before recommending that the idea proceed, be revised and retested, or be discontinued. In the laundry case, contribution is P20 an order, break-even is 100 orders, 80 orders loses P400 a month, and a profitable month can still be short of cash.
</Callout>
