## Begin with the customer's problem

The activity says it plainly: do not begin by choosing a product. Begin with the customer's problem. A good start-up idea is built on a problem that a particular group of people really has, and the first job is to define that problem before anyone designs a solution.

<Callout type="key">
The activity asks you to define the problem, not to solve it. Distinguish a problem from a proposed solution: "students need a delivery app" is a solution; "off-campus students arrive late because they depend on unreliable transport" is a problem.
</Callout>

## The five-part framework

| Part | What you do | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Identified problem and 4U | Identify three everyday problems in Botswana, choose one, and test it with the 4U framework | A chosen problem and a view of whether it is worth solving |
| 2. Customer segment | Isolate a narrow minimum viable segment (MVS) and profile it | Exactly who has the problem |
| 3. Problem evidence | Gather direct feedback or observations from people in the segment | Evidence of how severe the problem is, and the gaps that remain |
| 4. Customer problem statement | Write a structured statement using the formula | One clear sentence of cause and effect |
| 5. Problem qualification | Place the problem in BLAC and identify the white space | How the problem is perceived, and where there is room for a new offer |

### Part 1: identify the problem and apply the 4U test

Identify three problems experienced by people or businesses in Botswana. They may be about access to products or services, cost, delays or inconvenience, a poor customer experience, waste or inefficient processes, safety, health or environmental concerns, or unmet needs in a community or industry. The sheet suggests sectors such as university and student services, transport, water and energy, agriculture, tourism, retail and delivery, housing, waste management, health and wellness, and digital services. Choose the one your group finds most important.

<KeyTerm title="The 4U framework">
Four questions that test whether a problem may be worth solving. **Unworkable:** does the problem make it difficult for customers to complete an important activity? **Unavoidable:** is it difficult for customers to ignore or avoid? **Urgent:** do customers need it addressed soon? **Underserved:** the fourth U in the handout's framework.
</KeyTerm>

<Callout type="note" title="Check which version you were given">
The handout names four Us (Unworkable, Unavoidable, Urgent, Underserved). The Lecture 1 activity sheet gives questions for only the first three. The notes do not define Underserved with a question, so read it as asking whether customers' needs are inadequately met by what exists today, which connects to the white space idea in Part 5. If you are unsure, apply all four and say what you mean by the last.
</Callout>

### Part 2: define the minimum viable segment

<KeyTerm title="Minimum viable segment (MVS)">
The smallest group of customers who share similar characteristics and experience the same problem.
</KeyTerm>

Do not use broad labels such as "everyone in Botswana", "all students", "all businesses" or "all young people". Profile the segment on five parameters:

1. **Location**
2. **Occupation or role**
3. **Relevant behaviour**
4. **The situation in which the problem occurs**
5. **The common need or difficulty**

The sheet's own example of a clearer segment: second-year University of Botswana students who live off campus and depend on public transport to attend morning classes. It is guidance only; you may not use it as your group's problem.

### Part 3: collect evidence

Speak to at least three potential customers from the segment. Ask:

1. What difficulty do you experience?
2. When and where does it happen?
3. How frequently does it happen?
4. What consequences does it create?
5. How do you currently manage the problem?
6. What is unsatisfactory about the existing approach?

Record the answers without trying to persuade respondents to accept your preferred solution. Use direct feedback, qualitative quotes or observation to show how severe the problem is under the 4U test, and be open about the evidence gaps that remain.

### Part 4: write the problem statement

<KeyTerm title="Customer problem statement">
[Specific customer group] experiences [specific problem] when [situation or context], resulting in [measurable or observable consequence]. Evidence from our interviews indicates that [key finding].
</KeyTerm>

The sheet's example: small food vendors operating near offices in Gaborone struggle to estimate daily customer demand, resulting in food waste on some days and lost sales on others. The statement names the customer, the problem and the consequence, and it prescribes no solution. Do not copy the examples supplied.

### Part 5: qualify the problem with BLAC, and find the white space

The simplified BLAC analysis asks four questions, and your classification must be supported by interview or observation evidence. Do not assume every problem is blatant and critical.

| BLAC | The question to ask |
|---|---|
| Blatant | Is the problem already recognised by customers? |
| Latent | Is it not yet fully recognised? |
| Aspirational | Is it mainly desirable but not essential? |
| Critical | Is it critical enough for customers to seek a solution? |

<KeyTerm title="White space">
The open, underserved area for unique business positioning. Briefly explain what is missing from the customer's current alternatives, why the problem remains inadequately addressed, and why solving it may create a business opportunity. Use it in a limited way; do not develop a detailed competitive strategy yet.
</KeyTerm>

## What not to do yet

The activity sets firm boundaries. At this stage do not:

- develop the complete product or service;
- prepare a Business Model Canvas;
- design an MVP or prototype;
- conduct detailed internal and external analysis;
- select a competitive strategy;
- prepare financial forecasts; or
- conclude that the idea is feasible.

Those activities follow once the Chapter 3 concepts have been introduced. This activity is the starting point for developing start-up ideas, screening ideas and eventually assessing feasibility (Topic 7). Use a Botswana-based problem, make sure all members contribute, and be prepared to present.

## The submission and how it is marked

Groups submit a maximum of five slides: the identified problem, the customer segment, the problem evidence, the problem statement, and the problem qualification with the possible white space. The handout also asks for a proposed business idea statement on the last slide.

The rubric is worth 50 marks, 10 for each of five parts: the identified problem and definition (with the 4U framework), the customer segment, the problem evidence, the customer problem statement, and the problem qualification (BLAC classification and white space).

| Level | Marks | In general |
|---|---|---|
| Excellent | 8 to 10 | Specific and thorough, with clear local context and supporting evidence |
| Satisfactory | 5 to 7 | Right idea, but explanations are superficial or one or two elements are missing |
| Needs improvement | 1 to 4 | Vague or generic, jumps to a solution, relies on unverified assumptions, or copies the examples |
| Unattempted | 0 | Not attempted |

## Chapter 3 in the textbook

<Callout type="note" title="Textbook only">
The lecturer's activity is about defining the problem. Longenecker's Chapter 3 covers more, and the lecturer has said those concepts follow the activity. The chapter's objectives are to distinguish types and sources of start-up ideas, use innovative thinking to generate ideas, describe the external and internal analyses that shape which opportunities to pursue, explain broad strategy options and focus strategies, screen ideas, and assess feasibility.
</Callout>

- **Opportunity recognition and entrepreneurial alertness.** Opportunity recognition is identifying potential new products or services that may lead to promising businesses. Entrepreneurial alertness is readiness to act on unnoticed opportunities and to pivot quickly away from flawed first ideas. A good product or service serves important consumer needs, has recognised utility and benefits, and is affordable to a large group of customers.
- **Generating ideas.** The chapter lists twelve prompts: borrow ideas from other products or industries; combine two businesses into one; begin with a problem in mind; recognise a hot trend; improve a product's function; streamline a customer's activities; adapt a product to meet needs in different ways; imagine how the market could expand; offer products through a subscription; cash in on the sharing economy; make a product "green"; and watch new technologies. "Begin with a problem" is the one the lecturer's activity puts first.
- **Outside-in and inside-out analysis.** Outside-in analysis studies the venture's context: the general environment (factors influencing business in a society), the industry environment (factors affecting a business and all its competitors) and the competitive environment (competitors' strength, position and likely moves). Inside-out analysis assesses the business's own competitive potential through its **resources** (tangible and intangible), **capabilities** (routines that coordinate the use of assets) and **core competencies** (capabilities that give a competitive advantage). A **SWOT** analysis (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats) brings the two together.
- **Strategy.** A strategy is a plan of action that coordinates resources and commitments to achieve superior performance, and a sustainable competitive advantage is a value-creating industry position likely to last. The three broad options are cost-based, differentiation-based and focus strategies. A focus strategy restricts attention to one subset of customers, one product or service, or one region; a niche shields a firm from direct competition and allows unique expertise, but it can erode if competitors imitate, the segment disappears or loses its uniqueness, or new firms subdivide the industry.
- **Screening and feasibility.** Screening judges an idea by the strength of the idea, the industry and competitive advantage, the capability of the founders, capital requirements and venture performance, and the targeted market and customers. Feasibility analysis is a preliminary assessment of whether the venture is likely to succeed. A **fatal flaw** is a circumstance that could by itself make a new business unsuccessful, such as limited market potential, powerful competitors, a weak competitive advantage or high start-up costs. Management capability covers the fit between the venture and the leader's mission and comfort with risk, the leader's grasp of critical success factors and ability to execute, and the leader's connections to others essential to the venture.

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
Starting from a product or app instead of a customer problem, so the "problem" is really a solution in disguise.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Defining the customer as "students" or "everyone". An MVS needs all five parameters.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Presenting group assumptions as evidence. Without direct customer feedback or observation, the 4U claims and the BLAC classification are unsupported.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Writing a problem statement that omits the situation or the consequence, or that already contains the solution.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Going beyond the boundaries: building a Business Model Canvas, a prototype, financial forecasts or a claim that the idea is feasible.
</Callout>

## Summary

<Callout type="key">
Define the problem before the solution. Choose a Botswana problem, test it with the 4U questions, name a minimum viable segment on five parameters, gather evidence from at least three potential customers, state the problem as customer, problem, situation and consequence, and qualify it with BLAC and the white space. Leave the product, model, strategy and finances for later.
</Callout>
