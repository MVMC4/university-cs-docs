## Why entrepreneurship matters to Botswana

Entrepreneurship is bigger than starting a business. The deck names five contributions.

| Contribution | What it means |
|---|---|
| Employment | Businesses create jobs and livelihoods. |
| Diversification | New enterprises can broaden Botswana's economic base. |
| Innovation | Entrepreneurs identify problems and create solutions. |
| Citizen participation | Entrepreneurship lets people become producers, owners and employers. |
| Value creation | Successful businesses create value for customers, owners and communities. |

<Callout type="caution" title="Evidence first">
Longenecker's chapter uses South African examples and statistics. Do not present them as Botswana statistics. Before using a figure, ask three questions: What is the source? Is it current? Does it apply to Botswana? The manager's rule is that assumptions must be tested, which is the same discipline good entrepreneurship needs.
</Callout>

## Small business and entrepreneurial opportunity

<KeyTerm title="Small business">
A business that is relatively small compared with the largest firms in its industry. Operations are often geographically localised, financing usually comes from a limited number of individuals, and it may begin with one person but can grow beyond a one-person operation.
</KeyTerm>

<KeyTerm title="Entrepreneurial opportunity">
An economically attractive and timely opportunity that creates value for customers and owners.
</KeyTerm>

The two ideas are not the same. A small business describes the nature and size of a firm. An entrepreneurial opportunity describes what the firm is pursuing.

| | Small business | Entrepreneurial opportunity |
|---|---|---|
| What it describes | The nature and size of the business | An attractive, timely chance to create value |
| Example from the deck | A small takeaway selling chicken and chips | A convenient, affordable meal service designed around UB student needs |

The key question is whether the business is merely small, or whether it is pursuing an attractive opportunity.

<Callout type="note" title="Textbook">
The textbook words the definition as an opportunity that "creates value for interested buyers or end users". It counts a business as small when it has fewer than 200 employees in any sector, or fewer than 100 in agriculture. The older archived deck used "fewer than 100 employees". The textbook also defines **bootstrapping** as doing more with less in terms of resources invested, and, where possible, controlling resources without owning them.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note" title="Archived Topic 1 deck">
The 2022 deck separates the two kinds of owner. An **entrepreneur** has goals that include achievement, profit and growth, achieved through innovation and strategic management. A **small business owner** establishes a business primarily to further personal goals, including making a profit.
</Callout>

## From problem to opportunity

An opportunity starts with a problem, not with a product. The deck gives six steps.

<Steps title="Turning a problem into an opportunity">
  <Step title="Identify a problem or unmet need">
    Be specific. A general idea is not yet a problem.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Identify who experiences the problem">
    Name the customer.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Develop a solution">
    Only now think about what the business would offer.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Test whether customers value the solution">
    Value must come from customers, not from the founder's enthusiasm.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Test whether they are willing to pay">
    Valuing something and paying for it are different.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Build a business model that creates value sustainably">
    The value must last, not just appear once.
  </Step>
</Steps>

The whole entrepreneurial life, in the deck's words, runs from problem to need to opportunity to action to value to business growth to legacy. Entrepreneurship begins when we turn a meaningful problem into a value-creating opportunity.

## Three types of small firm

Classify a firm by the owner's goals and the firm's growth potential.

| Type | What it offers | Examples in the deck |
|---|---|---|
| High-potential venture | Designed for substantial growth | A Botswana technology business that can scale across SADC |
| Attractive small firm | Substantial financial rewards while staying relatively small | A specialist consultancy, premium catering or professional services |
| Micro or lifestyle business | Modest returns while supporting the owner's preferred lifestyle | A home bakery, a salon, a freelance service |

<Callout type="note" title="Archived Topic 1 deck">
The 2022 deck lists four types: the high-potential venture (a "gazelle"), the attractive small firm, the microbusiness and the lifestyle business, which permits the owner to follow a desired pattern of living. Class 1 combines the last two. Use the grouping your lecturer used when answering.
</Callout>

## Entrepreneurial qualities and risk

The deck lists seven qualities and attitudes:

- Big ego is not required.
- Commitment and determination.
- Leadership.
- Opportunity obsession.
- Tolerance of risk, ambiguity and uncertainty.
- Creativity, self-reliance and adaptability.
- Motivation to excel.

<KeyTerm title="Risk-taking is not gambling" tone="amber">
Gambling is committing resources without understanding the odds or the downside. Entrepreneurship is gathering information, testing assumptions, managing exposure and adapting. Before acting, ask three questions: What can go wrong? How likely is it? What can I do about it? A good entrepreneur is not fearless; they learn to manage uncertainty.
</KeyTerm>

<Callout type="note" title="Textbook">
The textbook's list of ways to fail as an entrepreneur: overestimate what you can do, lack an understanding of the market, hire mediocre people, fail to be a team player (usually from taking oneself too seriously), be a domineering manager, and fail to share ownership in the business equitably.
</Callout>

## Why people own businesses

The deck gives four motivations.

| Motivation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Personal fulfilment | Making a difference to customers, employees and communities |
| Personal satisfaction | Passion, challenge, achievement and recognition |
| Independence | Being your own boss and having greater control over your future |
| Financial rewards | Creating wealth through sound decisions and hard work |

Most owners have a mix. The deck asks whether any one motivation can become dangerous when taken to an extreme, for example a pursuit of money that tempts an owner to cut corners (see Topic 2).

<Callout type="note" title="Textbook">
The textbook describes kinds of entrepreneur. A **founder** (a "pure" entrepreneur) brings a new business into existence. A **second-stage entrepreneur** is an administrative entrepreneur who oversees an ongoing business. A **franchisee** is an entrepreneur whose power is limited by a contract with a franchising organisation. An **entrepreneurial team** is two or more people working together as entrepreneurs. A **reluctant entrepreneur** becomes one through severe hardship, and a **corporate refugee** becomes one to escape an undesirable situation.
</Callout>

## The competitive edge of small firms

A small firm can compete with a large one even though large firms may have more money, people, systems and market power. The deck names five potential strengths.

1. **Integrity and responsibility** build trust and reputation.
2. **Customer focus** means staying close to customers.
3. **Quality performance**: a small firm does not have to mean low quality.
4. **Innovation**: small firms adapt and experiment quickly.
5. **Niche markets**: serving a specific customer group exceptionally well.

In practice a small Botswana business competes through closeness to customers, faster decisions, faster adaptation of products and services, and a focus on a niche that is too small for a large firm to prioritise.

## Entrepreneurial legacy

The deck asks what you will leave behind:

- financial resources and business assets;
- jobs and people whose lives were improved;
- customers and communities served;
- knowledge, systems and opportunities created;
- a reputation for integrity, or the opposite;
- an example for the next generation.

<Callout type="note" title="Textbook">
The textbook slide on legacy mentions "winning the wrong game": the nature of the entrepreneurial endeavour reflects personal goals and values. The slide is brief, so treat the usual reading as an inference: you can succeed at something that was never worth doing. It also speaks of crafting a worthy legacy, meaning the tangible items and intangible qualities passed to heirs and society.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="mistake">
Treating a small business and an entrepreneurial opportunity as the same thing. Size describes the firm; opportunity describes the value it creates.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Quoting a South African statistic as if it described Botswana.
</Callout>

<Callout type="mistake">
Starting from a product idea instead of a customer's problem, and mistaking risk-taking for gambling or entrepreneurs for people without fear.
</Callout>

## Summary

<Callout type="key">
1. Small businesses are important to Botswana's economic and social development.
2. A small business and an entrepreneurial opportunity are not the same thing.
3. Entrepreneurs pursue opportunities and create value while managing risk.
4. Small firms compete through integrity, customer focus, quality, innovation and niche markets.
5. Think beyond starting a business: think about the legacy you want to leave.
</Callout>
